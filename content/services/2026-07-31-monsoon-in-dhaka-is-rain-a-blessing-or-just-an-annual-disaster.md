The sky turns dark gray and you feel the cool wind before the first drop falls. In a perfect world, this would be your sign to grab a hot cup of tea, look out the window, and enjoy the weather. But if you live in Dhaka, your first thought is usually much simpler: "How am I going to get home today?"   
  
Over 80% of central Dhaka is now covered in concrete and paved surfaces, leaving less than 8% green space and barely 5% water bodies. Out of more than 50 natural canals that once drained the capital into surrounding rivers, over half have been illegally filled in or choked off. The result? For anyone living here, a heavy rain means knee-deep water on the streets, endless traffic jams, sky-high rickshaw fares and sitting with your feet pulled up to avoid touching the dirty water.   
  
We see this every year, despite big budgets and new city projects. So why does a little rain turn the capital upside down every single time?   
  
1. Too Much Concrete, Not Enough Dirt  When rain falls on natural soil, the ground acts like a natural sponge and absorbs the water. In Dhaka, we have paved over parks, playgrounds, and open fields with thick layers of concrete and asphalt. Because the water cannot sink into the ground, 100% of the rainfall stays on top of the roads, instantly overwhelming the neighborhood streets. 

2. Blocked Canals and Lost Lakes  Decades ago, Dhaka relied on a natural web of canals (khals) like Kalyanpur, Katashekhal, and Dholaikhal to channel stormwater into the Buriganga, Turag, and Balu rivers. Today, many of these vital blue pathways exist only on paper. They have been covered by multi-story buildings, converted into box culverts, or filled with mountains of trash. When the natural outlets are blocked, water has nowhere to flow. 

3. The Plastic Problem  While city planning plays a huge role, daily habits make the situation much worse. Millions of single-use plastic bottles, polythene bags, and snack wrappers are thrown onto the streets every day. When it rains, this floating trash washes directly into the underground drainage grates. The plastic forms thick plugs inside the pipes, trapping the rainwater and forcing dirty sewage up onto the pavement. 

4. Changing Weather Patterns  The way it rains has changed. Instead of gentle rain spreading across an entire day, we now get sudden, intense bursts of heavy rain in a very short time. The city’s old drainage pipes simply cannot move that much water all at once.   
  
Where Do We Go From Here?   
Fixing this will take time, but the solutions are clear: 

● Restoring the 15 Critical Canals: Recent environmental research shows that excavating and restoring just 15 key encroached canals could solve up to 80% of Dhaka’s waterlogging problem. Unblocking these natural water routes would give trapped rainwater a direct exit path out to the rivers. 

●  Building a "Sponge City": Modern cities across Asia are replacing traditional hard concrete with permeable pavements, gravel trenches, and urban rain gardens. Designing sidewalks and public parks that absorb water directly into the ground can drastically reduce surface runoff. 

●  Neighborhood-Level Waste Traps: Installing simple trash-catchers at local drain openings and enforcing strict community bans on dumping plastic would stop drainage blockages before they even start. 

●  Creating Urban Wetlands & Retention Ponds: Every new housing project and city sector must be legally required to reserve open retention ponds to hold excess rainwater during peak downpours.   
  
Dhaka is our home, and rain should be something we enjoy, not something that paralyzes the city. With smarter planning and better daily habits, we can stop every shower from turning into a disaster.
