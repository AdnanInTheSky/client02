Often referred as the “**rainforest of the sea**”, coral reefs are vital marine ecosystem that support 25% of all marine life, protect over 150,000 kilometers of coastline from storms by absorbing up to 97% of wave energy, and provide food and economic lifelines for more than half a billion people worldwide. But this vital element of the sea is facing numerous threats putting them at risk of collapse. These threats include climate change, overfishing, pollution, and habitat destruction. **Climate change** is one of the biggest threats, as rising ocean temperatures and ocean acidification cause widespread bleaching and death of corals.

Coral bleaching is the process where corals turn completely white. It is a stress response in which corals lose the microscopic algae called **zooxanthellae** that live inside their tissues. These algae provide corals with much of their energy through photosynthesis and also contribute to their colors. When corals become stressed, they expel these algae and their white calcium-carbonate skeleton becomes visible through their transparent tissue. Now, the question is what causes the corals to lose these algae? Well, the primary trigger for coral bleaching is heat stress caused by **rising ocean temperatures** due to climate change. When ocean temperatures rise by just 1 to 2°C (1.8 to 3.6°F) above the normal summer maximum, the symbiotic zooxanthellae in coral tissues can damage the coral during the algae’s photosynthesis and causes the production of harmful oxygen molecules. In such situation, to protect itself, the corals evict the zooxanthellae into the water that leaves its tissues transparent giving them bleached white color.

Prolonged or repeated bleaching deprives corals of much of their energy that makes them more weak and more susceptible to disease and other environmental stresses. If severe stress continues for a long time, large numbers of corals may die. As a result, marine life flees the area due to a lack of food and shelter and nearby coastlines lose their natural barrier against storm waves.

Now, the question arises that why are coral reefs worth protecting? Because**** they hold enormous ecological, economic, and cultural value to hundreds of millions of people around the world, providing valuable ecosystem services, including nutrition, economic security, and protection from natural disasters. These underwater structures act as massive carbon sinks and provide food and shelter for over 7,000 marine species. They absorb wave energy, protecting vulnerable coastlines from severe storm damage and erosion. Local economies benefit enormously from billions of dollars generated annually through reef-based tourism. Furthermore, they support global fisheries by serving as critical nurseries for important fish species. These diverse habitats offer a unique pharmacy for developing breakthrough life-saving medicines. Despite their immense value, these fragile ecosystems face severe threats from human activity and climate change. Their loss would trigger a devastating collapse of marine food webs and coastal stability worldwide. Urgent global conservation efforts are needed to ensure their survival for future generations. These ecosystems are essential for environmental, economic, and cultural reasons. Protecting them is crucial for a sustainable future.

As suggested by the Great Barrier Reef Foundation, we need urgent action on climate change to drastically reduce global emissions, but this alone is not enough. Action on climate change requires a dual effort to protect our reefs. While the world works towards reducing greenhouse gas emissions, we must make coral reefs more resilient and help them adapt to the warmer temperatures already caused by climate change. We must remember that our everyday activities influence every stage of climate change. With every small and wise act, we can help controlling climate change, eventually reducing the ecological threat even deep down in the ocean.
