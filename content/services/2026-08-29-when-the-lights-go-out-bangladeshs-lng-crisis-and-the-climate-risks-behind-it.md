At nearly midnight, while writing this article in Mirpur, the lights went out. This was not an isolated power cut, rather recent gas shortages across Bangladesh eventually affecting ordinary l/ives. This exposes a deeper climate-energy vulnerability, exposing the consequences that can move through the national gas grid to power plants, workplaces and homes.  
  
**The Context: How Bangladesh Became More Dependent on LNG **  
  
Bangladesh has long relied on natural gas for electricity generation, industry, fertilizer, and households. As domestic production has struggled to meet growing demand, imported Liquefied Natural Gas (LNG) has become increasingly important. The Daily Star reports that LNG imports began in 2018 through Excelerate Energy’s FSRU, initially as a supplement to domestic production, but they have since become a critical pillar of the country’s gas supply.    
  
LNG is natural gas cooled to about minus 162°C, turning it into a liquid that can be transported by specialized ships. At a Floating Storage and Regasification Unit (FSRU), the LNG is converted back into gas and supplied to the national pipeline network.    
  
The process is simple: LNG ship → FSRU → National gas grid → Power plants, industries and households.      
  
**The** **Impact: How an Offshore Problem Affects Every Home **  
  
The current figures show the scale of Bangladesh’s vulnerability. In July 2026, a fire damaged the electrical cable systems at the Excelerate-operated FSRU off Moheshkhali, halting the supply of around 450 mmcfd of gas. When one regasification unit partially restarted, around 115 mmcfd initially returned to the national grid, according to Petrobangla officials quoted by Reuters. The disruption had already affected power generation, industry and households.    
  
The chain is straightforward:   
  
**Rough weather or technical disruption → LNG unloading or regasification is interrupted → less gas enters the national grid → gas-fired power plants receive less fuel → electricity generation falls → load-shedding and economic disruption increase. **  
  
According to the media reports, at least 62 generating units were facing fuel shortages after the pipeline gas supply dropped significantly following the LNG-terminal disruption. Of these, 26 were gas-fired generators, demonstrating how a problem at an offshore LNG facility can rapidly spread through the national electricity system.    
**This is where the climate threat becomes visible**. A disruption at sea can eventually become darkness in a home in Dhaka, reduced production in a factory and financial pressure on ordinary families.   
  
**More LNG Capacity, but More Resilience? **  
  
Bangladesh is moving towards expanding LNG capacity, including additional infrastructure around the Moheshkhali energy hub. More capacity can provide valuable operational support when one facility faces technical problems.   
  
However, the central question is **whether that infrastructure can withstand climate and weather disruptions. **
