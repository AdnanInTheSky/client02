At nearly midnight, while writing this article in Mirpur, the lights went out. This was not an isolated power cut, rather recent gas shortages across Bangladesh eventually affecting ordinary l/ives. This exposes a deeper climate-energy vulnerability, exposing the consequences that can move through the national gas grid to power plants, workplaces and homes.  

**The Context: How Bangladesh Became More Dependent on LNG **  

Bangladesh has long relied on natural gas for electricity generation, industry, fertilizer, and households. As domestic production has struggled to meet growing demand, imported Liquefied Natural Gas (LNG) has become increasingly important. The Daily Star reports that LNG imports began in 2018 through Excelerate Energy’s FSRU, initially as a supplement to domestic production, but they have since become a critical pillar of the country’s gas supply.    

LNG is natural gas cooled to about minus 162°C, turning it into a liquid that can be transported by specialized ships. At a Floating Storage and Regasification Unit (FSRU), the LNG is converted back into gas and supplied to the national pipeline network.    

The process is simple: LNG ship → FSRU → National gas grid → Power plants, industries and households.      

**The** **Impact: How an Offshore Problem Affects Every Home **  

The current figures show the scale of Bangladesh’s vulnerability. In July 2026, a fire damaged the electrical cable systems at the Excelerate-operated FSRU off Moheshkhali, halting the supply of around 450 mmcfd of gas. When one regasification unit partially restarted, around 115 mmcfd initially returned to the national grid, according to Petrobangla officials quoted by Reuters. The disruption had already affected power generation, industry and households.    

The chain is straightforward:   

**Rough weather or technical disruption → LNG unloading or regasification is interrupted → less gas enters the national grid → gas-fired power plants receive less fuel → electricity generation falls → load-shedding and economic disruption increase. **  

According to the media reports, at least 62 generating units were facing fuel shortages after the pipeline gas supply dropped significantly following the LNG-terminal disruption. Of these, 26 were gas-fired generators, demonstrating how a problem at an offshore LNG facility can rapidly spread through the national electricity system.  
**This is where the climate threat becomes visible**. A disruption at sea can eventually become darkness in a home in Dhaka, reduced production in a factory and financial pressure on ordinary families.   

**More LNG Capacity, but More Resilience? **  

Bangladesh is moving towards expanding LNG capacity, including additional infrastructure around the Moheshkhali energy hub. More capacity can provide valuable operational support when one facility faces technical problems.   

However, the central question is **whether that infrastructure can withstand climate and weather disruptions. **    
  
The International Energy Agency (IEA) warns that increasingly intense tropical cyclones can directly threaten coastal and offshore energy infrastructure through severe winds, heavy rainfall and storm surges, with sea-level rise adding further risks.    
This creates a critical question for Bangladesh: If several important LNG facilities remain exposed to similar weather conditions in the same coastal region, does additional capacity automatically create resilience? The answer is quite achievable. While more LNG capacity can increase supply, true resilience requires geographical diversification, stronger emergency planning and the ability to keep going when part of the system is taken out.   
  
**The fix: Resilience building Beyond LNG **  
Bangladesh does not have to give up natural gas overnight. The country still needs reliable power for homes, industry and economic growth. But what the current crisis proves is that energy security cannot be dependent on one fuel, one supply route or one vulnerable piece of infrastructure.   
  
The International Energy Agency (IEA) says building resilience to climate change in the energy sector is about identifying climate hazards, making infrastructure more resilient to shocks and preparing energy systems to absorb and recover from disruption.    
  
For Bangladesh, this means taking several concrete steps:    
1. Conducting climate-risk assessments before expanding coastal energy infrastructure   
2. Strengthening backup and emergency arrangements for LNG and gas supply and,   
3. Gradually reducing excessive dependence on imported gas through domestic renewable energy, energy efficiency and a stronger national grid.   
  
The goal should not simply be more megawatts or more LNG terminals. It should be an energy system capable of functioning when fuel imports are disrupted, weather becomes extreme and global markets become unstable.   
  
**The Call to Action: Climate-Proof Bangladesh’s Energy Future**   
Bangladesh’s energy debate must move beyond the question of how to produce more electricity. Policymakers must also ask whether the infrastructure behind that electricity can withstand the climate and weather risks of the future.   
  
**The next steps should be clear**, such as: making climate-risk assessments central to energy planning, strengthening backup and emergency systems, avoiding excessive concentration of vulnerable infrastructure and diversifying the energy mix through renewable energy, energy efficiency and a more resilient national grid.  
  
The current LNG crisis will eventually ease. Cargoes will arrive, terminals will recover and the lights may stay on again. But the warning should not disappear with the immediate shortage.  
For a country on the frontline of climate change, energy security cannot simply mean importing more fuel. It must mean building an energy system resilient enough to survive rough seas, cyclones, technical failures and future shocks.   
The real question is no longer simply whether Bangladesh can import enough LNG to keep the lights on today. It is whether Bangladesh is building an energy system resilient enough to keep the lights on when the next disruption arrives.
