Whenever people hear the word termite, they instantly think of it as the destroyer of furniture, eater of foundations, the cause of a wooden beam collapsing without warning. It is one of the few insects that has been the enemy of the house forever. However, within the past decade, research laboratories and drylands thousands of kilometres apart have come together to give us a very different idea of the termite, one in which this very same insect can be one of the more successful climate engineers in the world.

Some termite species erect massive mounds that look like great temples springing up from the world's savannas and drylands. But aside from their aesthetic appeal and incredible engineering, new research in *Science* finds that these structures, which can stand taller than five meters (17 feet) above ground, do something remarkable for the ecosystem: they hold back the desert. By providing a moist refuge in a dry landscape, termite mounds allow plants to flourish and keep desertification at bay. As such, these mounds could be key for mitigating some of the worst impacts of global warming in drylands. Some studies found a 70 percent difference in nitrogen levels, and an 84 percent difference in phosphorus levels, between termite mounds and surrounding soils. Additionally, termite mounds tended to be separated from one another by a distance of at least 65 feet, essentially spreading the beneficial effects across a vast landscape.

That anchor effect is exactly what caught researchers' attention in the savannas and drylands of Africa, South America, and Australia. A study published in the journal *Science* by a team of Princeton ecologists found that even if regional vegetation declines by 30 percent due to drought, plant cover stays steady on the termite mound. Given this, the researchers dub the termite mounds "islands of fertility."

Termites have also shaped the built environment, most famously through the Eastgate Centre in Harare, Zimbabwe. Architect Mick Pearce, working with the engineering firm Arup, modeled the building's ventilation on the self-regulating airflow inside termite mounds, which stay at a nearly constant internal temperature no matter how extreme the weather outside becomes. The result is a large office and retail complex that runs with no conventional air conditioning, uses roughly 35 percent less energy than comparable buildings in the same city, and saved millions of dollars in both construction and operating costs all while cutting the carbon emissions that come with running mechanical cooling around the clock. Nearly three decades after it opened, Eastgate is still cited as one of the clearest proofs that biomimicry can outperform brute-force engineering.

While Bangladesh isn't a classic dryland like the African savannas studied by the Princeton team, the country does have its own drought-stressed frontier: the Barind Tract in the northwest, spanning Rajshahi, Naogaon, and Chapainawabganj. The region receives only about 1,625 mm of rainfall a year against a national average of 2,550 mm, and its low soil water capacity, combined with heavy groundwater extraction, has pushed it into chronic climate-landscape stress. Recent vulnerability assessments found that nearly 29 percent of the Barind Tract faces extreme drought vulnerability, with groundwater depletion and low vegetation cover compounding the problem

Climate projections point to more frequent and severe drought specifically in this western region, and farmers there are already leaning on a range of adaptation strategies just to protect food, income, and livelihood security as conditions shift  This is precisely the kind of degraded, water-scarce landscape where the Princeton "islands of fertility" research becomes relevant not because Bangladesh needs to import African termite species, but because the underlying mechanism, soil engineers that concentrate moisture and nutrients into fixed points across a landscape, is a model that local ecological engineering and restoration projects in the Barind Tract could study or adapt, alongside the groundwater and afforestation-based adaptation work already underway in the region.
